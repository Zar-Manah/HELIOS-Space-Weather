Where Remote CME Forecasting Stops:
An Empirical Information-Ceiling Study Across Solar Cycles 23–25

Zar-Manah
Independent Researcher, Madrid, Spain
ORCID: 0009-0007-8443-8999  |  Permanent DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24406782
Abstract
Operational coronal mass ejection (CME) arrival-time forecasting has been constrained near a mean absolute error (MAE) of ten hours for over a decade, across more than thirty independent methods including three-dimensional magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) simulations on supercomputing clusters. This paper does not claim to break that barrier. Rather, it measures where the physical and informational ceiling lies, separating three factors commonly conflated in the literature: the forecasting model, the evaluation cohort, and the information available at launch.

Using a frozen catalogue of 43,559 CDAW CMEs, 610 matched CME–ICME associations, and an untouched Solar Cycle 25 evaluation cohort, we assess two frozen transit models across two frozen cohorts, reporting all four combinations. The central finding is that the cohort effect dominates the model effect by a factor of five: on the full Cycle 25 cohort (n = 126), the served model and the benchmark model are statistically indistinguishable (15.4713 h vs 15.4275 h; paired ΔMAE = +0.0438 h, 95% CI [−0.8367, +0.9117], p = 0.94), whereas on the clean one-to-one association subset (n = 91), both models improve by four to five hours (11.5792 h vs 10.5336 h). Published arrival errors near 10 h therefore reflect sample difficulty rather than algorithm superiority.

Furthermore, we demonstrate two observational ceilings: an empirical 90% conformal prediction interval cannot be narrower than 69.31 h (symmetric) or 64.63 h (asymmetric) at 88% nominal coverage (measured 74.04 h at 92.06% coverage), proving that 35 h intervals are information-infeasible. In addition, the launch-time Brier skill score for Dst ≤ −100 nT is bounded at 0.1500 even under oracle recalibration (measured 0.0768 against a target of 0.25). Placed in operational context against the CCMC CME Scoreboard, fully automated execution on a single consumer CPU core achieves 11.58 h on clean events, demonstrating competitive parity with complex operational pipelines while providing reproducible, mathematically certified uncertainty bounds.
Keywords: space weather, coronal mass ejection, arrival time, conformal prediction, information ceiling, reproducibility, solar cycle 25.

1. Introduction

Two numbers dominate the CME arrival-time literature: approximately ten hours of mean absolute error, and approximately twenty hours of standard deviation. Riley et al. (2018) analysed 724 forecasts from 32 models and found no statistically significant improvement over six years. Kay et al. (2024) re-examined the same scoreboard with corrected error measures and reported an uncertainty of order 10–15 h. A 2025 study of ambient-wind uncertainty (Raza et al. 2025) reached an identical premise: despite increasingly complex numerical and machine learning frameworks, the operational mean absolute error of remote CME arrival predictions still exceeds ten hours.

That plateau is rooted in fundamental physical limitations: a single white-light coronagraph viewpoint cannot constrain the along-sight component of the CME expansion velocity, the background solar wind speed and density along the Sun–Earth line remain unmeasured during transit, and the internal flux-rope magnetic configuration is unobservable until the ejecta reach the L1 Lagrange point. While these constraints are acknowledged in theory, they are routinely obscured in practice by selective cohort reporting and claims of "state-of-the-art" performance.

This study adopts an inverse, rigorous protocol. Rather than tuning models to cross arbitrary accuracy thresholds, we systematically measure the information ceiling supported by remote observations. The contributions are: (1) a complete four-cell evaluation design preventing selective reporting; (2) empirical decomposition of error into cohort vs model contributions via paired bootstrap; (3) quantitative proof of two information ceilings; and (4) an open, reproducible audit free of synthetic proxies.

2. Observational Data & Protocols

2.1 CME Catalogue and Associations

The underlying catalogue comprises 43,559 CMEs recorded by SOHO/LASCO (CDAW). Interplanetary associations are derived from the Richardson & Cane ICME list (325 one-to-one entries) complemented by 285 inferred matches, yielding 610 matched associations. The Solar Cycle 25 held-out evaluation cohort contains 126 positive arrival events with verified launch times, linear speeds, angular widths, and transit times within 12–150 h.

A second, restricted cohort consists of the frozen one-to-one subset (n = 91), corresponding exactly to the benchmark subset of earlier literature. Reporting both cohorts side by side exposes the selection bias inherent in historical comparisons.

2.2 Ambient Wind & Source Magnetism

In-situ background plasma and field parameters are taken from NASA OMNI2 at one-hour cadence (flow speed, proton density, dynamic pressure, Dst, and 24–36 h preconditioning). Source active-region magnetic invariants are ingested from SDO/HMI JSOC SHARP records (USFLUX, TOTPOT, R_VALUE, MEANSHAR, AREA_ACR), matching 3,758 events with 38.2% coverage of post-2010 active regions. All synthetic magnetic proxies used in legacy pipelines were explicitly purged and are gated by automated regression tests.

2.3 Temporal Causality

Every feature merged into the inference design matrix satisfies strict temporal causality (t ≤ t_forecast). Zero causality violations exist across all 41,756 unified feature rows.

3. Model Architecture

The framework consists of frozen artefacts that were never retrained for this study. The served model (transit_v3.joblib) is a heterogeneous ensemble combining LightGBM, XGBoost, ExtraTrees, and Ridge regressors, blended via inverse out-of-fold MAE in log-space. Inputs include launch velocity, angular width, 20 R⊙ speed, drag-based transit priors over a (w, γ) grid (Vršnak et al. 2013), and solar-cycle phase. Conformal prediction intervals are constructed via Conformalized Quantile Regression (CQR) with regime-dependent calibration.

The benchmark model (transit_v3_bench.joblib) employs non-negative least squares weighting trained strictly on the clean 91-event subset, utilizing absolute conformal residuals.

4. Empirical Results

4.1 Cohort Dominance Over Architecture

As detailed in Table 1 and Figure 1, the served ensemble achieves an MAE of 15.4713 h on the full cohort of 126 CMEs, while the benchmark model yields 15.4275 h. A paired bootstrap test (4,000 resamples on identical events) confirms that the two models are statistically indistinguishable on the unselected operational population:

ΔMAE = +0.0438 h, 95% CI [−0.8367, +0.9117], p = 0.94

Conversely, evaluating both models on the clean 1:1 subset (n = 91) reduces errors by 3.9 h (served) and 4.9 h (bench). Changing the evaluation cohort shifts accuracy by approximately 5 hours, dwarfing the 0.04 h difference between models. Any comparison across non-identical cohorts is therefore scientifically uninformative.

Furthermore, the apparent precision of the benchmark model on clean events fails out of cohort: its nominal 90% conformal coverage collapses from 87.91% to 78.57% on the full cohort, whereas the served CQR model maintains valid calibrated coverage (92.06%).

Figure 2: Empirical 90% Conformalized Quantile Regression (CQR) arrival-time intervals on the full Cycle 25 cohort (n = 126), ordered by observed transit time. Measured coverage is 92.06% with an average width of 74.04 h. The minimum static band achievable for 88% coverage is 64.63 h, proving that intervals narrower than 35 h are mathematically infeasible from remote observables alone.

4.2 The Conformal-Width Ceiling

Earlier specifications targeted a 90% prediction window narrower than 35 h. Figure 2 displays the actual calibrated intervals. The empirical mean width is 74.0419 h (coverage 92.06%). Mathematically, with residual transit dispersion of σ ≈ 15–21 h, achieving 88% coverage requires a span of ~3.1σ, corresponding to a theoretical minimum floor of 69.31 h (symmetric) and 64.63 h (asymmetric). Narrowing the window below 35 h without in-situ solar wind measurements is physically and mathematically impossible.

4.3 Apex Geomagnetic Skill Ceiling

For launch-time prediction of severe geomagnetic storms (Dst ≤ −100 nT), the measured Brier Skill Score (BSS) is 0.0768 (ROC-AUC 0.6980, 22 events). Recalibrating probabilities directly against the test labels yields an oracle ceiling of only 0.1500. A target skill score of 0.25 cannot be attained because the orientation of the southward magnetic field (Bz) is fundamentally unobservable in white-light coronagraph imagery.

5. Tactical Stage at L1

Once ejecta reach the L1 monitor, the physical regime shifts from remote estimation to in-situ boundary-value driving. Table 2 and Figure 3 present tactical Dst forecasting performance. 7. Discussion & Limitations

Several limitations remain intrinsic to remote space weather intelligence: (1) Single-view coronagraphs omit crucial line-of-sight kinematics; (2) Background solar wind along the Sun–Earth path is approximated from 1 AU L1 history rather than upstream heliospheric tomography; (3) The Cycle 25 cohort (n = 126) is modest, resulting in wide confidence intervals despite paired bootstrap reductions.

8. Conclusions

The persistent ten-hour barrier in CME arrival-time forecasting is not an algorithmic deficit, but a fundamental information ceiling governed by remote observables. Model architecture accounts for barely one hour of variance, while cohort selection accounts for five hours. Open science requires reporting all four cells, acknowledging information ceilings, and providing statistically guaranteed uncertainty intervals. HELIOS v3.4 provides a fully reproducible, open benchmark establishing the empirical boundaries of remote space weather forecasting.
